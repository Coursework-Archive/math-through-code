Question 7¶

(a.) Average velocity on an interval $[a,b]$ is $$ v_{\text{avg}}=\frac{s(b)-s(a)}{b-a}. $$

From the table (position in feet, time in seconds): $$ s(2)=20.6\ \text{ft},\quad s(3)=46.5\ \text{ft},\quad s(4)=79.2\ \text{ft},\quad s(5)=124.8\ \text{ft},\quad s(6)=176.7\ \text{ft}. $$

(ii.) Interval $[3,4]$: $$ \begin{aligned} v_{\text{avg}}[3,4] &=\frac{s(4)-s(3)}{4-3}\\ &=\frac{79.2\ \text{ft}-46.5\ \text{ft}}{4\ \text{s}-3\ \text{s}}\\ &=\frac{32.7\ \text{ft}}{1\ \text{s}}\\ &=32.7\ \text{ft/s}. \end{aligned} $$ $$ \boxed{v_{\text{avg}}[3,4]=32.7\ \text{ft/s}} $$

(iii.) Interval $[4,5]$: $$ \begin{aligned} v_{\text{avg}}[4,5] &=\frac{s(5)-s(4)}{5-4}\\ &=\frac{124.8\ \text{ft}-79.2\ \text{ft}}{5\ \text{s}-4\ \text{s}}\\ &=\frac{45.6\ \text{ft}}{1\ \text{s}}\\ &=45.6\ \text{ft/s}. \end{aligned} $$ $$ \boxed{v_{\text{avg}}[4,5]=45.6\ \text{ft/s}} $$

(iv.) Interval $[4,6]$: $$ \begin{aligned} v_{\text{avg}}[4,6] &=\frac{s(6)-s(4)}{6-4}\\ &=\frac{176.7\ \text{ft}-79.2\ \text{ft}}{6\ \text{s}-4\ \text{s}}\\ &=\frac{97.5\ \text{ft}}{2\ \text{s}}\\ &=48.75\ \text{ft/s}. \end{aligned} $$ $$ \boxed{v_{\text{avg}}[4,6]=48.75\ \text{ft/s}} $$

(b.) Use the graph of $s$ as a function of $t$ to estimate the instantaneous velocity when $t=3$.

Use average velocities on intervals closest to $t=3$:

$$ \begin{aligned} v_{\text{avg}}[2,3] &=\frac{s(3)-s(2)}{3-2}\\ &=\frac{46.5\ \text{ft}-20.6\ \text{ft}}{3\ \text{s}-2\ \text{s}}\\ &=\frac{25.9\ \text{ft}}{1\ \text{s}}\\ &=25.9\ \text{ft/s}, \\[6pt] v_{\text{avg}}[3,4] &=\frac{s(4)-s(3)}{4-3}\\ &=\frac{79.2\ \text{ft}-46.5\ \text{ft}}{4\ \text{s}-3\ \text{s}}\\ &=\frac{32.7\ \text{ft}}{1\ \text{s}}\\ &=32.7\ \text{ft/s}. \end{aligned} $$

A reasonable estimate for the instantaneous velocity at $t=3$ is the midpoint of these nearby slopes: $$ v(3)\approx \frac{25.9+32.7}{2}\ \text{ft/s} =29.3\ \text{ft/s}. $$ $$ \boxed{v(3)\approx 29.3\ \text{ft/s}} $$

(b.) Use the graph of $s$ as a function of $t$ to estimate the instantaneous velocity when $t=3$.

To estimate the instantaneous velocity at $t=3$, we consider the slope of the tangent line to the graph of $s(t)$ at $t=3$. Using the graph of the data points $$ t=[0,1,2,3,4,5,6], \quad s=[0,4.9,20.6,46.5,79.2,124.8,176.7], $$ a tangent line drawn at the point $(3,46.5)$ has slope approximately $29.7$.

Thus, the instantaneous velocity at $t=3$ is estimated to be $$ \boxed{v(3)\approx 29.7\ \text{ft/s}}. $$

Question 11¶

$\displaystyle f(x)= \begin{cases} e^x, & x \le 0,\\ x-1, & 0 < x < 1,\\ \ln(x), & x \ge 1 \end{cases} $

Given the piecewise function we determine the values of $a$ for which $\lim_{x \to a} f(x)$ exists.

Each expression defining $f$ is continuous on its interval. Therefore, $\lim_{x \to a} f(x)$ exists for all $a$ in the interior of each interval. The limit can fail to exist only at the transition points $x=0$ and $x=1$.

At $x=0$: $$ \lim_{x \to 0^-} f(x)=e^0=1, \qquad \lim_{x \to 0^+} f(x)=0-1=-1 $$ Since the one-sided limits are not equal, $\lim_{x \to 0} f(x)$ does not exist.

At $x=1$: $$ \lim_{x \to 1^-} f(x)=1-1=0, \qquad \lim_{x \to 1^+} f(x)=\ln(1)=0 $$ Since the one-sided limits are equal, $\lim_{x \to 1} f(x)$ exists.

$$ \boxed{\lim_{x \to a} f(x)\ \text{exists for all } a \neq 0} $$

Question 6¶

Evaluate the limit: $$ \lim_{t \to 7}\frac{3t^2+1}{t^2-5t+2} $$

By the Quotient Law, provided the denominator limit is nonzero, $$ \lim_{t \to 7}\frac{3t^2+1}{t^2-5t+2} = \frac{\lim_{t \to 7}(3t^2+1)}{\lim_{t \to 7}(t^2-5t+2)} $$

Numerator

Using the Sum Law and Constant Multiple Law, $$ \lim_{t \to 7}(3t^2+1) = \lim_{t \to 7}(3t^2)+\lim_{t \to 7}(1) = 3\lim_{t \to 7}(t^2)+1 $$

Using the Power of $x$ Law and Constant Law, $$ =3(7^2)+1=148 $$

Denominator

Using the Difference Law, Sum Law, and Constant Multiple Law, $$ \lim_{t \to 7}(t^2-5t+2) = \lim_{t \to 7}(t^2)-\lim_{t \to 7}(5t)+\lim_{t \to 7}(2) $$

Using the Power of $x$ Law, Identity Law, and Constant Law, $$ =7^2-5(7)+2=16 $$

Validity of the Quotient Law

Since $$ \lim_{t \to 7}(t^2-5t+2)=16\neq 0, $$ the Quotient Law applies

$$ \boxed{\frac{37}{4}} $$

Question 10¶

(a.) What is wrong with the equation $$ \frac{x^2+x-6}{x-2}=x+3 \, ? $$

Factor the numerator: $$ x^2+x-6=(x+3)(x-2) $$ So for $x\neq 2$, $$ \frac{x^2+x-6}{x-2}=\frac{(x+3)(x-2)}{x-2}=x+3 $$ But at $x=2$ the left-hand side is undefined (division by $0$), while $x+3$ is defined. So the equation is not true “for all $x$”; it is only true for $x\neq 2$.

$$ \boxed{\text{The left side is undefined at }x=2,\text{ so it cannot equal }x+3\text{ there.}} $$

(b.) Explain why $$ \lim_{x\to 2}\frac{x^2+x-6}{x-2}=\lim_{x\to 2}(x+3) $$ is correct.

Because the two functions are equal for all $x$ sufficiently close to $2$ (except at $x=2$ itself): $$ \frac{x^2+x-6}{x-2}=x+3 \quad \text{for }x\neq 2 $$ $$ \boxed{\text{Limits only depend on values near the point, not at the point, so the limits agree.}} $$ $$ \lim_{x\to 2}\frac{x^2+x-6}{x-2} =\lim_{x\to 2}(x+3)=5 $$

Question 16¶

Evaluate (if it exists): $$ \lim_{x\to 4}\frac{x^2+3x}{x^2-x-12} $$

Factor $$ \frac{x^2+3x}{x^2-x-12} = \frac{x(x+3)}{(x-4)(x+3)} $$

Cancel the common factor $(x+3)$ (valid when $x\neq -3$) $$ \frac{x(x+3)}{(x-4)(x+3)} = \frac{x}{x-4} \quad (x\neq -3) $$

Now check one-sided behavior as $x\to 4$

(a.) Left-hand limit $$ \lim_{x\to 4^-}\frac{x^2+3x}{x^2-x-12} = \lim_{x\to 4^-}\frac{x}{x-4} $$

As $x\to 4^-$, we have $x\approx 4>0$ and $x-4<0$, so $\frac{x}{x-4}\to -\infty$

$$ \lim_{x\to 4^-}\frac{x^2+3x}{x^2-x-12}=-\infty $$

(b.) Right-hand limit $$ \lim_{x\to 4^+}\frac{x^2+3x}{x^2-x-12} = \lim_{x\to 4^+}\frac{x}{x-4} $$

As $x\to 4^+$, we have $x\approx 4>0$ and $x-4>0$, so $\frac{x}{x-4}\to +\infty$

$$ \lim_{x\to 4^+}\frac{x^2+3x}{x^2-x-12}=+\infty $$

Since the one-sided limits are not equal, the two-sided limit does not exist $$ \boxed{\lim_{x\to 4}\frac{x^2+3x}{x^2-x-12}\ \text{DNE}} $$

Question 26¶

Evaluate (if it exists): $$ \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\dfrac{1}{-2+h}-\dfrac{1}{-2}}{h} $$

Question 38¶

Evaluate: $$ \lim_{x\to 0}\sqrt{x^3+x^2\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi}{x}\right)} $$

For all $x\neq 0$, we know $$ -1\le \sin\!\left(\frac{\pi}{x}\right)\le 1 \quad\Rightarrow\quad x^2\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi}{x}\right)\le x^2 \ \ (\text{since }x^2\ge 0) $$ So $$ x^3+x^2\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi}{x}\right)\le x^3+x^2=x^2(x+1) $$

Check continuity at the junction points

At $x=0$

Left-hand limit $$ \lim_{x\to 0^-} f(x)=\lim_{x\to 0^-}(x+2)=2 $$

Right-hand limit $$ \lim_{x\to 0^+} f(x)=\lim_{x\to 0^+}e^x=e^0=1 $$

Since $$ \lim_{x\to 0^-} f(x)\ne \lim_{x\to 0^+} f(x) $$ the two-sided limit $\lim_{x\to 0} f(x)$ does not exist

Also $$ f(0)=e^0=1 $$

$$ \boxed{\text{Discontinuous at } x=0} $$

At $x=1$

Left-hand limit $$ \lim_{x\to 1^-} f(x)=\lim_{x\to 1^-}e^x=e $$

Right-hand limit $$ \lim_{x\to 1^+} f(x)=\lim_{x\to 1^+}(2-x)=1 $$

Since $$ \lim_{x\to 1^-} f(x)\ne \lim_{x\to 1^+} f(x) $$ the two-sided limit $\lim_{x\to 1} f(x)$ does not exist

Also $$ f(1)=e^1=e $$

$$ \boxed{\text{Discontinuous at } x=1} $$

$\displaystyle f(x)= \begin{cases} x+2, & x<0,\\ e^x, & 0\le x\le 1,\\ 2-x, & x>1 \end{cases} $
